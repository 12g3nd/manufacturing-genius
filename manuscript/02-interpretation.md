The comparison matters a lot here. Under these assumptions, letting players
choose their risk can't pick the better player more often than making them both
play safe. The setup decides that direction; the simulation only tells you how
big the difference is. Compare strategic choice with making everybody gamble,
though, and it can come out better *or* worse. It would be pretty easy to blur
those two together and walk away with a much bigger claim than the model
supports, so I'm trying hard not to.

A winner doesn't have to cheat, or even misjudge the odds, to end up with a
score that overstates their ability. They can understand the rules perfectly
and play them well. The prize is for coming first, after all. The trouble starts
when the evaluator asks that same result to tell them who's actually better at
the underlying thing, because those aren't quite the same question. I think
that gap is worth paying attention to, but this study can't tell you how big it
is at any real firm, fund or hiring panel.

Before anyone takes this as an argument against risk, look at what risk is
allowed to do here: nothing. It adds no expected performance. Nobody learns
anything, works harder, or takes on a project that only pays off if someone is
willing to gamble on it. The safe rule gets a clean second read on ability, and
the players know things about each other that the evaluator can't see. Those
assumptions are generous to playing safe, and they're the reason the conclusion
has to stay attached to this particular game.
